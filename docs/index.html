How well does AlphaFold know a plastic-eating enzyme?

PETase, from the bacterium Ideonella sakaiensis, breaks down PET, the plastic in drink bottles. I tested AlphaFold's prediction of its 3D shape against every lab-measured PETase structure I could find, checked whether the AI knows which parts it's unsure about, and ran a blind test on newer relatives of the enzyme.

By Rayyan Niyazi. All data is public. The analysis code is my own (PowerShell and C#).

Summary

I compared AlphaFold's prediction of PETase with 26 lab-measured copies of the wild-type enzyme from 13 crystal structures. Its median gap to them (0.257 Å) is the same as the median gap between structures from different crystals (0.257 Å), and this held up in four different ways of doing the comparison. Where AlphaFold is less confident, the lab structures really are more flexible (Spearman ρ = 0.80, 95% CI 0.74–0.84). Its clearest confident error is at one residue, Ser193. It predicts the most common position of the enzyme's "wobbling" tryptophan, but not the alternative positions some crystals captured. For three related enzymes whose AlphaFold models are dated before their lab structures existed, it stays accurate (0.34–0.42 Å in the structured core).

1Background

A protein is a chain of amino acids that folds into a precise 3D shape, and the shape decides what it does. Measuring a shape in the lab, usually by X-ray crystallography, takes months. AlphaFold, an AI system from Google DeepMind, predicts a shape from the amino-acid sequence alone, in minutes.6 Its predictions are freely available in the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database.7

PETase was discovered in 2016 in a bacterium living on plastic waste1, and its first crystal structures followed in 2017–18.2–4 Since then, structural biologists have tested how far AlphaFold's predictions can be trusted. Large assessments found the predictions are as useful as experimental structures for many tasks, as long as the confidence scores are read critically.8 But even very confident predictions can differ from experiments in local details.9 Whether a low confidence score reflects real flexibility or just the model's uncertainty is still debated.10 This study tests all three of these questions on one well-characterised enzyme family.

Three measures come up throughout this study:

  • RMSD (root-mean-square deviation): line two structures up as well as possible, then average how far apart matching atoms are. It's measured in ångströms (Å). A carbon–carbon bond is about 1.5 Å, so 0.3 Å is about a fifth of a bond.
  • pLDDT: AlphaFold's own confidence score for each amino acid, from 0 to 100. Above 90 is very high, and below 50 means it's essentially guessing.
  • B-factor: recorded in every crystal structure. It measures how much each atom smears out, meaning how much that part of the protein wobbles.

2Method

  1. Got PETase's 290-amino-acid sequence from UniProt (A0A0K8P6T7) and AlphaFold's prediction from the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database (entry AF-A0A0K8P6T7-F1, database model version 6, produced by the AlphaFold Monomer v2.0 network). All data was downloaded on 21 November 2026.
  2. Searched the Protein Data Bank for every PETase structure (57 entries). The database marks 16 as having no mutations, but checking every sequence letter by letter showed three of them aren't wild-type: an ancestral reconstruction (8CRU, 28 differences), a computationally stabilised design (8D1D, 13) and a mutant (6KY5, 10). I kept only chains identical to UniProt over residues 34–290. That's 13 crystal structures, and 26 chains, since several crystals hold more than one copy.
  3. Structures use different numbering schemes and carry extra "tag" residues, so I matched every chain to the UniProt sequence by sequence alignment. Only identical amino acids were compared.
  4. Compared the 256 residues present in every chain (34–289). I lined up each pair on its backbone C-alpha atoms using the quaternion superposition method, and calculated RMSD, per-residue wobble across the 26 chains (RMSF), and B-factors normalised within each chain.
  5. Repeated the main comparison three other ways to check it doesn't depend on one choice: one chain per crystal, one crystal per research study, and each pair lined up on all the residues it shares.
  6. For the family and the blind test, I searched the Protein Data Bank for other PET-degrading enzymes (enzyme class 3.1.1.101). I compared each one with PETase and with its own AlphaFold prediction.
  7. Reported correlations are Spearman rank correlations across residues, with 95% confidence intervals from 2,000 bootstrap resamples.

4Against every lab structure

A single comparison could be luck. Lab structures also differ from each other a little, because of crystal packing, temperature and resolution. So the real question is whether AlphaFold falls inside the normal spread between experiments.

5Does AlphaFold know where it's unsure?

Every residue in the core scored between 95.8 and 98.9, so AlphaFold is very confident everywhere. But do its small dips line up with the parts that really move? I compared them with two independent measures of movement: how much each residue varies across the 26 lab chains (RMSF), and the crystal B-factors.

Lab wobble across 26 chains (RMSF)AlphaFold's distance from the average lab structure
  • Yes, strongly. AlphaFold's uncertainty correlates with crystal B-factors (ρ = 0.80, 95% CI 0.74–0.84) and with wobble between structures (ρ = 0.80, 0.74–0.84). The two lab measures correlate with each other at ρ = 0.86 (0.82–0.89), so AlphaFold's confidence tracks real flexibility nearly as well as one experimental measure tracks another.
  • This matches a large 2025 study that found pLDDT "reasonably correlates" with flexibility measured by simulations, NMR and B-factors.10 The same study warns that pLDDT misses flexibility caused by binding partners. PETase works by binding a plastic chain, and the prediction here is of the enzyme on its own.
  • Its errors are mostly in the moving parts. Where AlphaFold differs from the average lab structure correlates with lab wobble (ρ = 0.49, 0.39–0.58). Its largest error, at Asn73 (0.80 Å), is in the most flexible loop of the enzyme.
  • One confident mistake of its own: Ser193. Here AlphaFold is 0.76 Å from the average lab structure, its second-largest error, even though the 26 crystals agree closely (wobble 0.18 Å). It's off from every single crystal (by 0.36–0.93 Å), so this isn't one odd structure. It isn't a flipped backbone either: AlphaFold's backbone angles there are within about 15° of the crystals'. The chain is simply shifted slightly. AlphaFold is still very confident at this residue (98.6), so this is the clearest place where its confidence score doesn't warn of the error. This is the kind of local, high-confidence difference that crystallographers have reported for AlphaFold in general, and it's why they call predictions "valuable hypotheses" rather than replacements for experiments.9

6The wobbling tryptophan

Trp185 lines the groove where PET binds. Papers that number the enzyme without its signal peptide call it W156. Crystallographers described it as "wobbling" between positions and linked this to how the enzyme grips its substrate and releases its product.2,5 Simulations of related enzymes suggest the wobbling tryptophan also changes how it stacks against the plastic's ring during the reaction.11 I measured the angle of its ring (the side-chain angle χ2) in every chain.

Lab chainsAlphaFold
  • AlphaFold predicts the most common position (92°). But a prediction is one snapshot. It can't show that this ring moves between positions. The movement only shows up when many experiments are compared.

7PETase's family

I found 11 other PET-degrading enzymes with lab structures, mostly from soil and compost bacteria. How similar are they to PETase?

  • Shape is conserved more than sequence. Evolution has changed about half of the amino acids while keeping the fold and the active site. This is a classic pattern in biology, and it's a big part of why AI can learn protein shapes at all.
  • The tryptophan is kept almost everywhere. Ten of eleven relatives have a tryptophan at the position of Trp185. That fits its suggested role in holding the plastic.5,11

8A blind test

AlphaFold was trained on structures released before 30 April 2018, and PETase's own structures came out in 2017–18. So it may have effectively seen the answer. Six of the relatives were first solved in 2022–2025, after training. There's a second catch, though. When the database builds a model, it can also use existing structures as templates. So I checked the date on each model file. Three models (for 7QJO, 8GZD and 9O9W) are dated June 2022, before their own lab structures were released, so they couldn't have used them. I call these strictly blind. The other three models are dated 2025, after their structures were public.

All residuesStructured core (residues within 2 Å)
  • Still accurate on enzymes it couldn't have seen. For the three strictly blind enzymes, the core RMSD is 0.34–0.42 Å. For all six newer enzymes it's 0.28–0.42 Å, against 0.27–0.32 Å for the older ones. That's slightly worse, but still close to the 0.257 Å typical gap between two crystals of the same protein.
  • Only three strictly blind cases. That's enough to show AlphaFold isn't simply copying a known answer, but too few to say how accurate it is in general.
  • The two big misses were flagged in advance. For 7QJT and 9O9W the overall RMSD is about 1.4–1.5 Å. That's entirely from the last 4–8 residues of the chain, which AlphaFold had marked with low confidence (averages of 59 and 66). Read with its confidence scores, the prediction never misled.

9Limitations

  • The blind test is blind to the protein, not to the family. AlphaFold learned from the older relatives in this table and can use them as templates, so "unseen" here means a new member of a known family. A protein from an unknown family would be a harder test.
  • Crystals aren't the whole truth. Every lab structure here is a crystal, so it's frozen, packed and often cooled. In solution the enzyme moves more than any of these show.
  • Only C-alpha atoms are used for the main RMSDs. That measures the backbone fold. Side chains, which matter for binding plastic, can differ more (see Trp185).
  • A handful of residues are missing. The shared core covers 256 of the 257 residues seen in the crystals. Lining up each pair on all its shared residues raises the lab median by 0.01 Å and AlphaFold's by 0.035 Å, which is still within the experimental range (Table 1).
  • B-factors aren't pure flexibility. They also capture disorder in the crystal itself, so they're an imperfect stand-in for how the enzyme moves.10 I used them alongside the variation between structures, which gave a similar answer.
  • Neighbouring residues aren't independent. Amino acids next to each other in a loop tend to move together, so the confidence intervals on the correlations are probably a little too narrow.
  • The structures aren't independent either. Five of the 13 crystals come from one study under similar conditions. Counting them once changes the medians by less than 0.03 Å (Table 1).
  • B-factor refinement differs between labs. Some groups use methods (such as TLS) that change how B-factors spread across a chain. Normalising within each chain reduces this, but doesn't remove it.
  • No plastic bound. The prediction and most of the crystals show the enzyme on its own. How well AlphaFold handles PETase gripping a PET chain isn't tested here.

10Part 2: a second AI, and an engineered enzyme

Two follow-up questions, answered with the same 26 wild-type crystal chains and 256 core residues.

Is AlphaFold unusual, or would any predictor do this well?

I ran ESMFold, a different structure predictor that works from a protein language model rather than from aligned families of sequences, on the mature PETase sequence using the public ESM Metagenomic Atlas service.

  • ESMFold is good, but about half as precise. Its error is roughly double AlphaFold's and sits outside the experimental range. Its confidence scores also track flexibility less well. So AlphaFold's match with the crystals isn't something any modern predictor achieves automatically.
  • Both predictors are off at Ser193 (ESMFold by 0.64 Å). When two different methods make the same "mistake", it's worth asking whether the crystals share something unusual there, such as the way molecules pack against each other in the crystal. That's an open question I can't answer with this data.

Can a predictor see what FAST-PETase's mutations do?

FAST-PETase is a version of PETase with five mutations (S121E, D186H, R224Q, N233K and R280A), found with machine learning, that breaks down PET much faster.13 Its crystal structure is 7SH6. I compared the real structural change (7SH6 against the wild-type crystals) with the change ESMFold predicts (ESMFold's FAST-PETase against its wild-type).

  • The mutations barely move the backbone. FAST-PETase's crystal is a median of 0.255 Å from the wild-type crystals, the same as wild-type crystals are from each other (0.257 Å). At the five mutated positions, the backbone moved by 0.08–0.34 Å, at most 1.7 times the normal wobble there. No residue anywhere moved more than three times its normal wobble.
  • The predictor agrees there's almost nothing to see. ESMFold predicts an overall change of just 0.09 Å, and its residue-by-residue changes barely match the real ones (ρ = 0.25, 0.13–0.36).
  • So the speed-up isn't in the backbone's shape. Whatever makes FAST-PETase faster has to come from things these comparisons don't measure, such as side chains, surface charge, stability at higher temperatures or how the enzyme meets the plastic. Backbone structure prediction, however accurate, isn't the right tool for spotting it.

What I learned

For a well-studied enzyme family, AlphaFold's predicted shape is as good as a second experiment, and its confidence score is a reliable guide to which parts to trust. What it doesn't give you is movement: the wobbling Trp185 only showed up by comparing many experiments. Part 2 showed that not every predictor reaches this level, and that a famous engineered variant doesn't differ from the original in backbone shape at all. Next, I'd test a protein from a family with no known structures, and look at side chains and stability, where enzymes like FAST-PETase and the improved LCC used for bottle recycling12 probably gain their advantage.
